# Sligo (Working Note)

Sligo and the wider south Sligo Atlantic coast as a story location: a politically abandoned but radiologically marginal stretch of the northwest, used by the Ireland character for occasional sail-trip salvage runs. Specifically: **Streamstown townland on the south Sligo coast, near Aughris Head and Ballysadare Bay** — a cluster of abandoned coastal cottages on exposed Atlantic ground where personal-scale salvage happens outside the Catholic network's reach.

## Sligo's Status at Year 12

### Contamination

Sligo lands in a useful gap in the established zone map. Working through `ireland--fallout_research.md`:

- **Not in the permanent exclusion zone proper.** That is the six counties plus the named border counties (Donegal, Leitrim, Cavan, Monaghan, Louth). Sligo borders Donegal and Leitrim and sits at the immediate southern edge — the research file says "the boundary is not sharp — contamination grades southward."
- **Not in the Waterford-Limerick band.** Sligo is well north of that.
- **Not in the safe zone** as defined by population concentration and Catholic-network authority (Kerry, Clare, parts of Tipperary).
- **Atlantic-facing, not Irish Sea.** This is the structural advantage. Sligo Bay opens west into the clean Atlantic. Sellafield's contamination of the Irish Sea does not reach Sligo by water. The fallout research credits prevailing westerlies with keeping the southwest safe; the same weather keeps Sligo's air comparatively clean.

Net: **radiologically marginal but liveable.** Patchy, lighter than Dublin or Wexford, not lethal. A geiger counter would flicker; nobody would die quickly from being there. Long-term residents over twelve years would carry the same general elevated-cancer-risk surcharge as any post-collapse population. The Atlantic fishery off Sligo is in principle viable in a way the Irish Sea is not.

### Political status

Sligo is **abandoned by the Catholic zone**, not because it was poisoned but because no one was going to defend that far. The network drew its perimeter at Kerry, Clare, and parts of Tipperary on grounds of population, defensibility, and the natural northern wall of the Waterford-Limerick band. Sligo got left on the wrong side of the line.

Practical consequences:

- No parish authority, no confession-style sessions, no doctrinal enforcement, no ration system.
- No salvage tax, no inspection of incoming goods.
- No protection. Whoever is in Sligo is on their own with whoever else is in Sligo.
- The only routine contact from the south is the occasional small boat coming up the west coast for trade or salvage.

### Population — the third option

The northern-refugee map (`map_descriptions.md` §1b) names Galway, Clare, and Kerry as the rejection points. Sligo is not on that list. Sligo Bay is the **first** west-coast bay a boat from Donegal reaches after rounding Malin Head. With no Catholic-zone authority there to turn anyone away, some refugee boats plausibly stopped and stayed.

Working assumption (flagged): Sligo at year 12 contains a small mixed remnant of perhaps a few hundred to low thousands —

- **Original Sligo residents** who didn't leave when the leaving was happening. Old people who wouldn't walk, families with too many children to move, individuals who chose not to.
- **Northern Irish Protestants** off boats that came around Malin Head and stopped at the first habitable bay rather than push on to Galway and the rejections.
- **A few rejected refugees** from further south who walked back inland after Galway turned them away.
- **Drifters** — people who left somewhere worse and ended up here.

This remnant is quiet, mixed, and politically below the radar. Not a faction. Not a polity. A surviving, scattered population on a coast nobody is fighting over.

This makes Sligo the **third option** in the post-collapse Irish-political geometry: not Catholic safe zone, not French Cork, not exclusion-zone refugee. A fourth thing.

The Ireland character's trips to Sligo are partly trips into this third option. He may or may not meet any of these people on a given run. The evidence of them — a smoking chimney across a field, a maintained garden, a footprint, a door closed since last year, **a light burning across the bay at dusk where there should be none** — is enough.

### Power — hydro at Ballysadare, solar around the bay

The Sligo remnant has electricity. This is the most consequential fact about them and inverts the political map.

**Hydro at Ballysadare.** The Ballysadare River drops a real waterfall at Ballysadare town at the head of the bay. Pre-collapse the falls drove the Pollexfen mills through the nineteenth century — water-powered flour milling on a substantial scale, an existing piece of Irish industrial history. After collapse, hydro is the most reliable renewable in the landscape: it works in January in the rain, it requires only river flow, the mechanical components are repairable with hand tools. A small Pelton wheel or crossflow turbine on the falls with a permanent-magnet generator produces continuous kilowatt-scale power year-round. The water has not stopped.

This is most plausibly a community-scale installation — not the original Pollexfen mill restored, but a modern micro-hydro setup on the same falls, either pre-collapse infrastructure that was never decommissioned or a remnant-built rig assembled from salvaged components in the years after.

**Solar around the bay.** Pre-collapse rural Ireland had scattered domestic solar — never universal, but enough that twelve years later the remnant has panels to consolidate. PV panels are the most durable post-collapse renewable component: 25-year manufacturer warranties, often producing at degraded output for 30–40 years. Inverters die at 8–15 years and get scavenged off neighbouring rooftops to keep working sets running. Lithium batteries last 10–15 years and have probably had one full replacement cycle from salvage by year 12. A small community pooling kit can maintain a working solar setup as summer surplus on top of winter hydro.

**Combined output.** Hydro is the winter base load; solar is the summer peak. The combination produces enough for: lighting, refrigeration (medicine, fish, milk), radio transmission and reception, small workshops, hand-tool charging, a fish-smoking shed running an electric blower instead of bellows. It does not produce enough for: heating, electric vehicles, computer farms, anything industrial.

**The structural inversion.** The abandoned coast has working power. The Catholic safe zone in Kerry/Clare does not — its surviving solar is patchy, there is no hydro of significance, and the population centres run essentially pre-electric (candles, oil lamps, fires, hand tools). **Sligo has more electrical capacity than the political winners.** This is the inverse of what the political map would suggest, and it is a fact the parish would prefer not to think about.

**What this enables, and what it does not.**

- A radio at Ballysadare. The remnant can listen to whatever still broadcasts on long-range bands and can transmit if they choose to. Whether they choose to is a separate question — broadcasting is also being located.
- A refrigerated fish catch. The Atlantic fishery off Sligo is in principle the most valuable in Ireland; combined with refrigeration it is genuinely productive. The catch can be held, smoked at leisure, traded south.
- Lighting at night. A handful of windows lit on the south side of the bay after dark is the visible signature of the remnant from offshore. The character on his bar-wait may see them.
- It does *not* enable computers, neural-implant infrastructure, satellite contact, AI systems. Working PV-and-hydro at this scale is small-community electrification, not technological civilisation. The remnant is industrially-aware, not technologically active.

**Pollexfen continuity.** Yeats's mother Susan Pollexfen grew up around the Ballysadare mills. Yeats wrote about Ballysadare repeatedly; "Down by the Salley Gardens" — the salley (willow) gardens of Ballysadare — is one of his early signatures. A post-collapse remnant restoring water-power on the same falls is a literal continuity with pre-industrial Ireland: the mills powered the area for over a century, electrification killed them, collapse has brought water-power back. McCarthy-clean prose would not name Yeats or the Pollexfens — but the structural fact is there for the reader who knows.

**The Catholic zone's relationship to all this.**

Open question with several plausible answers:

- *Tolerated discreet presence.* Both sides know about each other and quietly ignore the asymmetry. Sligo doesn't broadcast. The parish doesn't send envoys.
- *Unknown to the parish.* The parish believes Sligo is empty. The Ireland character, who has been there, knows otherwise. He has not told them.
- *Acknowledged but unmentioned.* The leadership knows. The doctrine doesn't. Information is held tightly.

The third is the most novel-shaped. It puts the character in the position of carrying an inconvenient fact across a doctrinal line.

## Geography for the Novel

### Sligo Bay system

Sligo Bay opens west to the Atlantic and is divided internally into three arms by sandbars and islands:

- **Drumcliff Bay** — the northern arm, under Ben Bulben. Drumcliff churchyard (Yeats's grave) sits on the road just inland.
- **Sligo Harbour proper** — the centre, where the Garavogue river enters the sea. The town sits at the head, reachable by small boat up the river. Its own bar at the entrance.
- **Ballysadare Bay** — the southern arm, behind Strandhill peninsula and Knocknarea. **This is the bay the chapter uses.** Wide, shallow, broad sand exposed at low tide, treacherous for navigation without local knowledge.

The dominant landscape feature of the wider system is **Ben Bulben**, the flat-topped mountain rising over Drumcliff Bay on the north. **Knocknarea**, with the Neolithic cairn of Maeve, dominates the south side and is the relevant mountain for the south-coast chapter — it sits behind Strandhill and is the closer, looming landmark when seen from Aughris and Ballysadare Bay. Ben Bulben from this angle is across the bay to the north-northeast: visible on a clear day, distant, looking like what it is rather than looming.

### The south Sligo coast — Aughris and Ballysadare Bay

This is the chapter's actual ground. The exposed Atlantic shore on the south side of Sligo Bay, west of Strandhill, running through small townlands toward the Mayo border.

Coast detail (east to west, the way a boat from the south approaches it in reverse):

- **Strandhill** — peninsula between Sligo Harbour and Ballysadare Bay. Surfing waves on the open side, sand bars in the bay. Knocknarea rises directly behind it.
- **Ballysadare Bay** — the southern arm of the Sligo Bay system. Broad and shallow. Its mouth is closed by a bar that requires high tide for safe entry. Inside the bay are wide sand flats; the Ballysadare river enters at the head of the bay.
- **Beltra and the inner bay** — small communities along the southern shore.
- **Aughris Head** — coastal headland on the open Atlantic, west of Ballysadare Bay's mouth. Cliffs, a small historic pier (Aughris Pier), Aughris Strand. Pre-collapse there was a famous pub here (the Beach Bar) — a substantial stone building that may still stand. Tiny pre-collapse population.
- **Templeboy, Skreen, Easkey** — small parishes and coastal townlands continuing west.
- **Enniscrone and Killala Bay** — further west again, into Mayo.

### The bar at Ballysadare Bay

The chapter's bar is the **Ballysadare Bay bar**, not the Sligo Harbour bar. Its character:

- The bay is shallow and broad — at low tide vast sand flats are exposed and the bay is essentially unnavigable. Locals refer to it as a place where the sea goes out to the horizon.
- The entrance bar must be crossed near high water. A boat arriving at the wrong tide anchors outside in deeper water and waits.
- Spring tide range in Sligo Bay is around four metres. A wait can be six hours, sometimes longer in marginal weather.
- Shelter while waiting: the lee of Aughris Head if the wind is right, or the lee of the Strandhill peninsula on the north side. Atlantic-exposed otherwise.
- At high water the bar fills and the bay opens up. The crossing goes with the flood; inside is calmer water but still wide-shallow with sand banks demanding attention.
- Westerlies are the danger. An Atlantic gale across the bar makes the crossing impossible until it dies; a boat can be pinned for days, watching the coast but unable to land.

The bar is a **liminal scene location**. The wait outside it — six hours of nothing while looking at an empty Atlantic coast with Knocknarea behind it and Ben Bulben distant across the bay — is one of the McCarthy-clean scenes the novel has been waiting for.

If the wait runs into evening, the chapter has an option that activates the power thread above: **lights coming on at the head of Ballysadare Bay** — a few of them, low and yellow, where on the political map there should be nothing. The Pollexfen falls hydro is producing as it has been all day; the difference is now visible. The character watches them from the boat. He may or may not say anything to the crew about what they are. The crew may or may not already know.

### Aughris Pier as alternative landing

If Streamstown is reached more easily from the open coast than from inside Ballysadare Bay, **Aughris Pier** is a viable landing point and changes the chapter slightly:

- Small historic pier on the open Atlantic, west of Ballysadare Bay.
- Less tide-dependent than the bay itself but more weather-exposed — a westerly swell makes Aughris a difficult landing.
- A boat could put in here on a calm day and the salvage party walk inland to Streamstown.
- The Beach Bar building — pre-collapse pub, stone-built, robust — might be a landmark of the landing scene. Possibly itself salvaged or used as shelter by passing crews.

The choice between **bar-crossing into Ballysadare Bay** and **landing at Aughris Pier on the open coast** is a chapter-shape choice. The bar gives a longer wait scene and a quieter inland feel. Aughris gives a more exposed landing and a more direct walk to the cottages. Both are usable.

### The literary geography (adjusted for the south coast)

Ben Bulben and Yeats's grave at Drumcliff are across the bay to the north — visible from Aughris on a clear day, but not the chapter's foreground. The chapter's mountain is **Knocknarea**, which is older, pre-Christian, and carries different weight:

- **Knocknarea** — flat-topped, with the great Neolithic cairn of Queen Maeve at the summit. Visible behind Strandhill from anywhere on the south Sligo coast. Older than anything Christian. An iron-age presence that doesn't need explaining.
- **Aughris Cliffs** — local landscape feature on the headland, exposed Atlantic.
- **Ben Bulben** — across the bay, northeast, distant. Visible but peripheral.
- **Yeats's grave at Drumcliff** — also across the bay, further inland. Not part of this chapter; potentially part of a later one.

This is a useful shift. Ben Bulben and Yeats are too literary to anchor every Sligo scene; Knocknarea and Maeve give the south-coast chapter an older, less authored landscape. The Yeats register can be saved for a later chapter that goes north across the bay.

## Streamstown

A coastal townland on the south Sligo Atlantic coast, in the Aughris / Ballysadare Bay area (Templeboy / Kilmacshalgan parish ground). Walkable from a small-boat landing on Ballysadare Bay or from Aughris Pier on the open coast.

### What's there

A cluster of three or four — perhaps a few more — abandoned rural cottages on or near the coast. A stone wall or two, a path up from the strand or in from the headland. Slate-roofed whitewashed dwellings of the kind that line the south Sligo coast. Some intact, some sagging, some gone. Twelve years of Atlantic weather. Doors blown open. Windows mostly broken but some panes still in place.

This is genuinely remote ground. Pre-collapse Streamstown had a tiny scattered population — a handful of farming families, occasional summer visitors, a coastal walking route. After twelve years it is quieter than that. The nearest substantial pre-collapse landmark is the Beach Bar at Aughris (a stone-built pub, possibly still standing, possibly used as occasional shelter by passing crews). The nearest pre-collapse settlement of any size is Strandhill or Ballysadare town, both several kilometres east.

### Why this location

- Walkable from Ballysadare Bay or Aughris Pier — small-boat landing, no town to navigate
- Far from any remnant settlement; empty on most days
- Atlantic-exposed enough to be left alone by larger salvage operations from the south
- Specific enough that the character has a reason to go *there* rather than anywhere else — possibly because a previous trip showed him something he wants to come back for, possibly because of a family-name connection to the townland, possibly because the Beach Bar stone is a recognisable landfall and Streamstown is a short walk from it

### What can be salvaged from a year-12 abandoned cottage

What survives twelve years of Atlantic weather depends on whether the roof held.

**Likely intact (if dry)**:
- **Glass.** Window panes where frames have held; framed picture glass on parlour walls; mirrors (especially over fireplaces); cabinet glass; pressed-glass dishes. **This is the prize for the Ireland character.** Old cottage glass is sometimes hand-rolled or imperfect, distinctive optically — exactly what his art practice would want. A specific parlour mirror or a particular pane is a genuine reason to sail two days for.
- **Cast iron.** Range, kettle, pots, pans, fire-irons. Indestructible, valuable. He may or may not take it; the parish would.
- **Hand tools.** Hammers, saws, planes, sickles, billhooks. Every cottage had a yard with tools.
- **Crockery.** Willow-pattern china, mugs, jugs, dresser plates. Decorative survivors and useful ones.
- **Religious objects.** Sacred Heart picture, Pope photograph, holy water bottle on the windowsill, rosary on a nail, missal on the dresser. The Catholic zone has a real market in salvaged devotional items.
- **Books.** Family bibles, missals, school readers. A Yeats volume is plausible in Sligo.
- **Bottles.** Whiskey bottles in the press (mostly empty, occasionally sealed). Patent medicines in cupboards — paregoric and laudanum-grade preparations from older households, occasionally still sealed. Ties to the year-12 pharmaceutical-decline thread (`substance_culture_research.md`).
- **Linen and wool.** If kept dry, blankets and linen survive. If damp, black with mould.
- **Photographs.** Behind glass on dressers and walls. Sepia weddings, communions, a son in uniform, a daughter in New York.

**Likely rotted**:
- Soft furnishings, mattresses, curtains, untreated clothing, paper not behind glass, untreated leather.
- Wood near broken windows.
- Food except sealed tins.

**Easy to miss but structurally significant**:
- A specific painting on a parlour wall — a small Jack Yeats print, a Paul Henry coastal scene, an unsigned watercolour someone's aunt did. Behind glass. The glass and the image both interest him.
- Sheet music in a piano stool. (The piano's strings and frame are worth taking; the case is firewood.)
- Letters tied with string in a drawer.
- Tools specific to a trade — a fiddler's spare bridge, a weaver's bobbins, a thatcher's needles.

### The garden — substance-research interlock

Cottage gardens in Sligo at year 12 have been self-seeding for over a decade undisturbed. Atlantic climate suits perennials. What grows:

- **Opium poppy** (*Papaver somniferum*). Self-seeded for twelve years in the gentle climate of Sligo. Ties directly to the parish-poppy thread in `substance_culture_research.md` — a man pocketing a seed-head from a Streamstown garden is doing something the doctrine has views about. The reader who knows sees it; the reader who doesn't sees a man picking flowers.
- **Cottage herbs** — rosemary, thyme, fennel, sage, mint, comfrey. Perennial and abundant.
- **Surviving fruit trees** — apple, pear, damson. Untended but bearing in season.
- **Foxglove, valerian, St John's Wort** — plant medicine running wild in old gardens.

The garden is the chapter's quiet pharmaceutical scene. Whatever else he carries off the boat at Streamstown, a few seed-heads in a cloth bag is part of it.

## How the Catholic Zone Reaches Sligo

### The sail trip

From Kerry or Clare to Sligo Bay:

- North up the west coast: Dingle / Kerry coast, past the Aran Islands and into Galway Bay, north past Connemara, Achill, Clew Bay, Mayo coast, into Sligo Bay.
- Distance: roughly 250–350 km depending on departure port. Multi-day passage in a small sailing boat. Genuinely dangerous Atlantic water.
- This is the same coast the northern refugees sailed in the early years (`map_descriptions.md` §1b), in the opposite direction. The character is retracing their route.
- The journey passes Galway — a rejection point for the refugees. The character would see what is left of it as he passes. The boat would not stop there; nobody on the route stops anywhere they don't have to.

### Who makes the trip

Working assumption: small-crew expeditions. Not a regular trade route — these are infrequent, personal or semi-official runs. Possible crew compositions:

- Catholic-zone fishermen who do this occasionally for trade
- A French Cork crew (the northern-refugee descendants integrated into Cork have maritime skills and might be available for this kind of work)
- A trader who ranges the west coast and takes passengers

The character's relationship to the boat and crew matters to the chapter's politics. A French-Cork-northern-Irish hybrid crew taking a Catholic-zone artist up to Sligo is exactly the kind of cross-territorial human texture this novel wants. They share the boat for days, eat together, weather-watch together, share what they share — a drink, a smoke, occasional words across the dialect line.

### Frequency

Open question. Plausible patterns:

- **Annual.** A summer trip when weather permits. He goes once a year for what he needs.
- **Less than annual.** A trip every two or three years for specific objectives.
- **Occasional and specific.** No fixed pattern; he goes when something draws him.

The annual or every-two-year pattern fits a year-twelve character with established habits. The specific-and-occasional pattern fits a man whose practice is obsessive rather than routine.

## Story Uses

### As a chapter set piece

The Sligo trip is a clean self-contained chapter unit:

1. **Outbound passage** — handled in white-space or compressed; not the chapter.
2. **Approach and wait at the bar** — anchored outside, six hours of falling-then-rising tide, Ben Bulben in and out of cloud, crew silent or near-silent. The McCarthy register at full strength.
3. **The crossing** — bar fills, boat goes over with the flood, inside is calmer.
4. **Going ashore at Streamstown** — beach the boat or anchor in a small inlet, walk up.
5. **The work inside the cottages** — slow, attentive, specific objects rendered one at a time, no commentary.
6. **A small turn** — see options below.
7. **The return** — carrying what he came for and what he didn't expect, down to the boat, the tide is right, they go.
8. **Inbound passage** — again compressed.

### Possible chapter turns (in order of force)

- **An object that knows him.** A photograph he recognises. A name in a missal. A handwriting he's seen before. He isn't a stranger here — he's somewhere connected to him, possibly without knowing it. This binds the trip biographically.
- **They're not alone.** A footprint that wasn't there an hour ago. A door that was closed and is now open. A thread of smoke from a chimney across the field. The Sligo remnant population is real; he is in their houses. He doesn't necessarily meet anyone. The evidence is enough. This sets up later chapters.
- **Lights across the bay.** During the bar-wait at dusk, lights come on at the head of the bay. Yellow, low, electric. The remnant's power infrastructure made visible. He has seen them before or he hasn't; either way the chapter rests on a single shot of light where there should be none.
- **A working object.** Inside one of the Streamstown cottages, a powered light socket that comes on when the switch is thrown. Or an old radio that hisses when the dial is turned. The grid reaches further than he thought; the cottage is on someone's line. He turns it off again.
- **A still in the back shed.** The cottage isn't entirely abandoned. Someone is using it. Recent, careful. Substance-research interlock.
- **A burial.** A small mound in the garden with a slate stone. Twelve years on. Whoever lived here finished here.

The strongest single answer is probably the second — they're not alone — because it gives Sligo its named place in the novel as the third option and creates structural payoff for later chapters where he might come back, or where someone from Sligo might come south.

### As a recurring location

If Sligo holds, it can carry more than one chapter:

- A first run that establishes the bar, Streamstown, and the cottages.
- A second run, later in the novel, where the third-option remnant becomes a meeting.
- A possible final use as a refuge or escape route — Sligo is outside parish authority, which makes it valuable if the character ever needs to be unfindable.

### As an answer to the parish's reach

Every chapter the character spends in Sligo is a chapter outside the Catholic network's authority. He is not in confession. He is not under doctrinal observation. He is in someone else's parlour twelve years after they left. This gives the novel a recurring escape valve from parish institutional pressure — the kind of breathing space the tone guide's anti-pattern check (avoiding moralising religious authority) wants on the page rather than only in negative.

## Open Questions

- **Bar-crossing into Ballysadare Bay or landing at Aughris Pier on the open coast?** Both are viable. The bar gives a longer wait scene and a quieter inland feel; Aughris gives a more exposed landing and a more direct walk to the cottages.
- **Does the character know whose cottages these are?** Family-name connection, childhood-visit memory, parish-archive reference, or chosen for tactical reasons? The biographical answer is more powerful.
- **The Beach Bar.** Is the pre-collapse pub building at Aughris still standing? Is it used (by remnants, by passing crews)? Is it a landmark or a setting in its own right? Possible quiet scene there: a man finding a stone-walled room with bottles still on shelves.
- **Is there a remnant population, and does he meet them?** First run: no, only traces. Subsequent runs: maybe.
- **Frequency of trips.** Annual, every two or three years, or occasional and specific.
- **Crew composition.** Catholic-zone fishermen, French Cork hybrid, trader-and-passengers. The crew is a small political fact in itself.
- **What he carries the salvage in.** Wicker creels, sailcloth bundles, a small handcart — the physical detail of the chapter lives here.
- **What he does NOT take.** The choosing is the chapter's quiet drama. What he leaves on the dresser may matter as much as what he carries.
- **Does the parish know about the trips?** Officially sanctioned for cover, privately personal? Or genuinely unknown to the network?
- **Does the parish know about the power?** Tolerated, unknown, or acknowledged-but-unmentioned. The third is the most novel-shaped. It puts the character in the position of carrying an inconvenient fact across a doctrinal line.
- **Does the Sligo remnant broadcast?** They have the capacity to transmit on long-range bands. Whether they choose to is a separate question. If they do, who is listening — the Catholic zone, the French Cork zone, the Wuyishan-collective Columban infrastructure, none of the above?
- **Is any of the power infrastructure pre-collapse remnant or post-collapse build?** The hydro at Ballysadare is plausible either way. A pre-collapse community-scale installation that was never decommissioned says something different from a post-collapse rig assembled by the survivors. The latter implies more skill on the ground than the parish would credit.
- **Does the power reach Streamstown itself?** The cottages are several kilometres from Ballysadare town. Long lines are possible but unlikely; spurs and isolated micro-installations are likelier. A single working light in a Streamstown cottage is a much sharper detail than general electrification.
- **Does anything from Sligo eventually reach the Wuyishan collective via the Catholic-network communication channel?** A Yeats line, a photograph, a cottage-garden seed in a sealed packet.
