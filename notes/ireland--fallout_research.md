# Ireland Fallout Research Notes

## What Official Studies Have Said

### Irish EPA Study (2016) — Severe Accident at Sellafield

The Irish Environmental Protection Agency assessed worst-case accident scenarios at Sellafield. Key findings:

- **No evacuations needed in Ireland** for an accident scenario
- People would receive approximately **twice their normal annual radiation dose**
- "Severe radiological effects in Ireland are unlikely"
- The main threat is **food chain contamination**: milk, meat, vegetables, cereals, rainwater
- Without food controls, annual radiation doses from contaminated food could exceed **370,000 μSv**, with increased cancer rates for decades
- Staying indoors during plume passage reduces exposure by up to 80%
- Estimated economic cost: **~€4 billion**
- Source: [EPA 2016 Report](https://www.epa.ie/publications/compliance--enforcement/radiation/potential-radiological-impact-on-ireland-of-postulated-severe-accidents-at-sellafield.php)

### RPII Assessment — Attack Scenario

The Radiological Protection Institute of Ireland stated that the impact of a **terrorist attack** on Sellafield was **"unquantifiable"** because the UK would not provide access to safety data. Without that data, no meaningful assessment of a deliberate strike could be made.

### Irish Government Briefing (2022, post-Ukraine invasion)

The EPA briefed the Irish government that a nuclear accident in the UK or Europe could **significantly contaminate Irish food** with radiation, contaminate water supplies, and cost ~€4 billion.

### Hinkley Point Emergency Planning

- Detailed emergency planning zone: **3.5 km radius** (evacuation)
- Wider food restriction zone: **30 km radius**
- EDF's own 2019 report: food restrictions recommended to **43 km downwind**

### Why the Story Scenario Is Far Worse Than These Assessments

The official studies model **accidents at a functioning facility with containment**. The story involves **military strikes during a global war**. The difference is enormous:

| Factor | Accident Scenario (EPA study) | Military Strike (story) |
|---|---|---|
| Containment | Partially intact | Completely breached |
| Source term | Limited release from one system | All stored waste exposed simultaneously |
| Sellafield plutonium stockpile | Protected in storage | Aerosolised by explosion and fire |
| Fire duration | Hours, with firefighting | Weeks, with NO emergency response |
| Simultaneous events | Single site | Sellafield + Hinkley Point + Liverpool |
| Emergency services | Functioning | Collapsed (global war) |
| Food controls | Implemented immediately | No functioning government to implement them |
| Population response | Orderly sheltering | Mass displacement, no coordination |

The EPA's "no evacuation needed" finding applies to an accident with emergency response. Strip away containment, firefighting, food controls, and government coordination — and add two additional contamination sources — and the outcome is qualitatively different.

The story's scenario is not "twice normal annual dose." It is persistent, unmanaged, multi-source contamination with no remediation infrastructure, no food testing, and no coordinated response. The contamination accumulates through the food chain unchecked.

This is why Northern Ireland becomes a permanent exclusion zone and why the Waterford-Limerick band stays unfarmable for decades: not because the physics are different, but because there is no one left to manage the consequences.

## UK Civil Defense: What Actually Exists

### Government Protection

- **Pindar bunker**: 200 feet beneath the Ministry of Defence in Whitehall. ~400 bunks. Ministers, senior military, key staff only. PM is the only person allowed to bring family.
- **Cold War regional bunkers**: 11 "Regional Seats of Government" were planned for officials. Not public shelters.
- After 2012, civil defense planning was moved to the Cabinet Office and conducted in secrecy. Local Resilience Forums handle peacetime emergencies but have no nuclear war mandate.

### Public Protection

- **Public nuclear shelters: None.** Zero. The UK has no public shelter infrastructure.
- **Switzerland comparison**: Switzerland maintains shelter capacity for ~114% of its population. The UK has capacity for ~400 government officials.
- The Civil Defence Corps was **disbanded in 1968** and never replaced.
- The UK government made a deliberate policy choice: invest in nuclear deterrence (Trident) rather than civil defense. The logic was that civil defense was futile against thermonuclear weapons, so the money was better spent on the ability to strike back.

### What the Public Actually Gets

- "Protect and Survive" (1974-1980): Cold War pamphlets advising people to shelter under doors, tape windows, and listen to the radio
- Updated to **"Go in, stay in, tune in"**: Move indoors, stay away from windows, seal vents, follow government updates on TV/radio/internet
- That's it. There is no shelter to go to. No evacuation plan. No distributed supplies. No underground infrastructure.

### Rural Population

- **Completely unprotected.** The advice is to shelter in your house.
- A rural farmhouse provides minimal radiation shielding compared to a concrete building
- Rural areas are further from any emergency response infrastructure
- If fallout drifts over agricultural land, there is no mechanism to protect crops, livestock, or water supplies in real time
- Farmers were identified by the Irish EPA as "key to protecting food" in a nuclear incident — meaning the plan relies on individual farmers pulling crops and killing livestock on government instruction, assuming government communications still function

### What This Means for the Story

- The total collapse of the UK population outside government bunkers is **realistic**. There is literally no infrastructure to protect them.
- Rural communities in northwest England (near Sellafield) and Somerset (near Hinkley Point) would have no warning, no shelters, and no coordinated response
- The "pray the fallout misses you" scenario is not dramatic exaggeration — it is the actual civil defense plan
- The contrast with Switzerland is telling: a country that invested in civil defense might survive a nuclear exchange with its population largely intact. The UK did not make that choice.
- This explains why the UK is so devastated in the story — it's not that the weapons were uniquely powerful, it's that there was nothing between the weapons and the people

## Sources of Contamination

### Sellafield (Cumbria, northwest England)

- Not a normal power station — one of the most significant nuclear sites in Europe
- Holds ~140 tonnes of separated plutonium (largest civilian stockpile in the world)
- Decades of spent nuclear fuel and legacy waste in deteriorating ponds and silos
- If struck, releases not just reactor-level isotopes but massive quantities of stored waste
- Key isotopes released: Cesium-137 (half-life 30 years), Strontium-90 (half-life 29 years), **Plutonium-239 (half-life 24,000 years)**, Americium-241 (half-life 432 years)
- Location: Directly across the Irish Sea from Dublin (~200km). The Irish Sea becomes a contaminated body of water.
- Prevailing winds are westerly (Ireland → England), BUT easterly events do occur and would push fallout directly at Ireland's east coast
- Even without direct wind-borne fallout, contamination of the Irish Sea itself devastates both coastlines

### Hinkley Point (Somerset, Bristol Channel)

- Active reactor site on the Severn Estuary
- Primarily Cesium-137 and Strontium-90 from reactor fuel (no major plutonium stockpile)
- Located ~300km from southeast Ireland (Waterford/Wexford)
- Under northerly or northeasterly winds, fallout crosses the Celtic Sea and makes landfall on Ireland's southeast coast
- The fallout deposits as it hits terrain: the hills inland from Waterford, the Shannon estuary around Limerick
- **This is the plausible mechanism for the Waterford-Limerick fallout band**

## Fallout Map for Ireland

### Northern Ireland — Permanent Exclusion Zone

- Sources: Sellafield plutonium + Liverpool contamination (from the escalation war)
- The plutonium from Sellafield makes this qualitatively different from other fallout zones
- Plutonium-239 half-life: 24,000 years. This is not a "wait it out" situation.
- Cesium-137 would clear in ~100-300 years, but the plutonium in the soil means no safe agricultural use within any human planning horizon
- Belfast, Derry, the border counties: Abandoned. Not coming back.
- The Irish Sea coastline (both sides): Heavily contaminated from Sellafield waste entering the water
- **Can people return? No. Not for farming. Not for generations. Possibly not ever in any meaningful sense.**

### East Coast of Ireland (Dublin, Wicklow, Wexford coast)

- Exposed to Irish Sea contamination from Sellafield
- Direct fallout from easterly wind events possible
- Plutonium deposition likely lower than Northern Ireland but present
- Coastal fishing: Dead. The Irish Sea is contaminated.
- Coastal farmland: Compromised for decades to centuries depending on deposition levels
- Dublin: Likely partially evacuated or severely depopulated; contamination patchy depending on rainfall during fallout events
- **Can people return? Some areas possibly in 30-50 years with monitoring. Coastal fishing: no.**

### The Waterford-Limerick Band — Contamination Belt

- Source: Hinkley Point fallout crossing the Celtic Sea under northerly/northeasterly winds
- Primarily Cesium-137 and Strontium-90 (no significant plutonium component)
- Deposited along a roughly east-west band as fallout hits rising ground inland
- Ireland's wet climate and peat soils are the worst case for cesium cycling (Welsh precedent: 26 years of sheep restrictions from Chernobyl fallout alone)
- At 12 years post-event: **Cesium still actively cycling in soil. Not safe for farming.**
- Estimated timeline for partial agricultural return: 30-50 years post-event (longer in peat areas, shorter in mineral/clay soils with remediation)
- Some people may live in the band but cannot safely farm — they are subsistence-level or dependent on supplies from the south
- **Can people return? Not yet at 12 years. Maybe in another 20-40 years for mineral soil areas. Peat zones: 50-100+ years.**

### South of the Line — Safe Zone

- Below the Waterford-Limerick deposition band, fallout levels drop sharply
- Kerry, Cork, Clare (south of the Shannon), Tipperary (southern portions), and the southwest coast: Largely clean
- Mild climate, agricultural base, low population density, rainfall from Atlantic (clean westerly weather)
- This is where the functioning Irish society exists
- **Safe for farming. Safe for habitation. The agricultural backbone of surviving Ireland.**

## Chernobyl Precedent (real-world data)

- Chernobyl fallout reached Wales (~2,000km away) in 1986
- 53,000 hectares of upland farms affected; 300+ farms restricted; 180,000 sheep under monitoring
- Restrictions lasted **26 years** (lifted June 2012) — and this was from distant, diluted fallout
- Cause of persistence: Peaty upland soils keep cesium bioavailable; it cycles from soil → grass → sheep → soil indefinitely
- Ireland's soils and climate are very similar to upland Wales: wet, peaty, grass-based pastoral farming
- Conclusion: Ireland's contaminated zones would behave like Welsh uplands but worse, because the source is closer and the deposition heavier

## Wind Speeds and Transport Times

### What wind speeds are needed to carry fallout from the UK to Ireland?

Short answer: **Nothing unusual.** Normal Irish Sea weather is sufficient. The distances are short and typical wind speeds easily cover them.

### Distances

| Source | Target | Distance |
|---|---|---|
| Sellafield → Dublin | ~200 km across Irish Sea |
| Sellafield → Belfast | ~250 km |
| Liverpool → Belfast | ~220 km |
| Liverpool → Dublin | ~230 km |
| Hinkley Point → Waterford | ~300 km across Celtic Sea |
| Hinkley Point → Limerick | ~380 km |

### Typical Wind Speeds (Met Eireann data)

- Ireland's average wind speeds: 3–8 m/s (11–29 km/h) depending on location
- Coastal and Irish Sea speeds higher: 8–12 m/s (29–43 km/h) common
- Gale conditions (frequent in autumn/winter): 17+ m/s (62+ km/h)
- Fallout modelling typically uses 24–72 km/h (15–45 mph) wind speeds

### Transit Times at Typical Winds

| Route | At 20 km/h (light) | At 40 km/h (moderate) | At 60 km/h (gale) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sellafield → Dublin (200 km) | 10 hours | 5 hours | 3.3 hours |
| Sellafield → Belfast (250 km) | 12.5 hours | 6.3 hours | 4.2 hours |
| Hinkley Point → Waterford (300 km) | 15 hours | 7.5 hours | 5 hours |
| Hinkley Point → Limerick (380 km) | 19 hours | 9.5 hours | 6.3 hours |

### Key Points for the Story

- No extreme weather event is needed — ordinary wind carries fallout across the Irish Sea in hours, not days
- Prevailing winds are from the south and west (Ireland → England), which would normally protect Ireland
- **But:** Easterly winds occur regularly, especially February to May. An easterly event during or after the strikes would push Sellafield fallout directly at Ireland's east coast and inland
- A northerly or northeasterly wind event would push Hinkley Point fallout across the Celtic Sea to Waterford/Limerick
- The deposition is worse where rain falls — if an easterly wind carrying fallout coincides with rain over Ireland (very likely given Ireland's climate), the contamination is washed out of the air and deposited heavily on the ground
- **Rain + easterly wind = maximum deposition.** This is exactly what happened with Chernobyl fallout over Wales in 1986.
- Nuclear fallout from weapon strikes extends 300–1,200 km downwind in real modelling (Nevada tests). The UK-to-Ireland distances (200–380 km) are well within this range.
- The fallout plume is not a single pulse — Sellafield's stored waste would burn and release contamination over days or weeks, meaning multiple wind events would distribute material in different directions over time

### What This Means for the Map

- The contamination pattern is shaped by which wind directions occurred during and after the strikes
- Northern Ireland gets hit from multiple angles: Sellafield (east), Liverpool (east/southeast)
- Dublin and east coast: Sellafield on easterly winds, plus Irish Sea contamination
- The Waterford-Limerick band: Hinkley Point on northerly/northeasterly winds
- The southwest (Kerry, Cork): Protected by distance, prevailing westerlies, and the fact that Atlantic air is clean
- It is realistic and doesn't require any unusual weather — just ordinary wind variation over a few days

## Power and Energy in Post-Collapse Southern Ireland

### At the Moment of Collapse

- ~60% of Irish farmers own a generator (ICMSA survey data from 2025)
- ~40% do not — even after major storms, many farms had no backup power
- Most farm generators are PTO-driven (tractor-powered) or standalone diesel/petrol units
- Very few rural homes are genuinely off-grid capable (solar + battery, micro-hydro, or small wind)
- Ireland's national grid was already vulnerable pre-collapse (supply-demand margins narrowing, 30% of demand going to data centres)

### First Weeks to Months

- Diesel/petrol generators run until fuel runs out — days to weeks depending on stored supplies
- PTO generators require a running tractor, which requires diesel — same problem
- No resupply chain. Once stored fuel is gone, generator-based power is finished.
- Solar panels (where they exist) continue working but are rare on rural Irish farms and low-output in Irish climate
- A handful of small wind turbines on farms continue working
- National grid: dead and not coming back

### First Few Years

- Communities begin salvaging and repurposing:
  - Solar panels stripped from abandoned houses, commercial buildings, data centres
  - Small wind turbines redistributed from farms that were evacuated or depopulated
  - Car batteries, UPS systems, and any stored electrical components become valuable
- **Micro-hydro becomes the key technology.** Southern Ireland (Kerry, Cork especially) has excellent river systems with elevation drops
  - A micro-hydro turbine runs 24/7, needs minimal maintenance, and lasts 25-50 years
  - Power formula: 6 × head (metres) × flow (cubic metres/sec) = kilowatts
  - A 6kW system can power ~5-6 homes. A 75kW system can power ~75 homes.
  - Former water mill sites are the best locations — existing infrastructure, proven head and flow
  - Kerry and Cork have many former mill sites
  - Cork, Kerry, and Wicklow hold ~80% of Ireland's small-scale hydro potential

### At 12 Years Post-Collapse (Story Timeframe)

| Power Source | Who Has It | Reliability | Percentage of Population |
|---|---|---|---|
| Micro-hydro (community) | Settlements near rivers with head | 24/7, very reliable | ~15-20% |
| Salvaged solar | Scattered, individual/community | Daylight only, weather-dependent | ~5-10% |
| Small wind turbines | Exposed coastal/hilltop sites | Intermittent but frequent in Ireland | ~5% |
| Salvaged vehicle alternators + improvised water/wind drive | Improvised workshop setups | Low output, unreliable | ~5% |
| No electrical power | Everyone else | Fire, candles, animal/human labor | ~60-70% |

### Key Points for the Story

- **Most people have no electricity.** The majority of the surviving rural population (~60-70%) lives without any electrical power at 12 years. They use fire for heat, candles/oil lamps for light, and human/animal labor for work.
- **Power is a community resource, not an individual one.** Where it exists, it's shared — a micro-hydro turbine serving a cluster of homes, a salvaged solar array powering a community workshop. Nobody has their own private grid-equivalent supply.
- **Micro-hydro is the winner.** It runs day and night, rain is an advantage (more water flow), maintenance is simple, and the turbines last decades. Southern Ireland is one of the best places on Earth for this technology.
- **Power creates hierarchy.** Communities with reliable micro-hydro can run lights, charge batteries, power basic medical equipment, run communication gear. They become centres of gravity. Communities without power are darker, more isolated, more dependent.
- **The Ireland character's location matters.** If he's near a river with head (very likely in Kerry or Cork), his community may have reliable low-level power — enough for lights, a radio, maybe the equipment he uses for his art practice (music playback, lighting for the glass-and-painting work).
- **Data centres are ruins full of treasure.** Ireland hosted massive data centres pre-collapse. They contain solar panels, UPS battery systems, backup generators, cables, server racks (metal), cooling equipment. Salvaging these sites is probably an organised activity.

## Salvaging No-Go Zones

### The Chernobyl Precedent

People have scavenged the Chernobyl exclusion zone continuously since the late 1980s — cutting out heating systems, stripping cable, removing antennas, hauling scrap metal by the thousands of tonnes. "Stalkers" spend days or weeks in the zone across multiple trips. One documented stalker estimated spending a cumulative year inside the zone over 50+ visits.

Official safe limits at Chernobyl:
- **30km zone**: No more than 5 days without health harm
- **10km zone**: No more than 1 day
- **Red Forest / reactor proximity**: Hours at most; permanent stay exceeds occupational limits within days

But illegal scavengers routinely exceed these limits. The consequence is increased cancer risk over months to years, not immediate death (in most areas). It's a trade people make when the alternative is poverty or starvation.

### Zone-by-Zone Salvage Assessment for Ireland

#### Northern Ireland (Sellafield Plutonium + Liverpool Fallout)

- **The plutonium makes this qualitatively different from Chernobyl**
- Plutonium-239 is an alpha emitter: low danger from external exposure, but **lethal if inhaled as dust**
- Any scavenging that disturbs surfaces — opening doors, moving rubble, walking through buildings — kicks up plutonium-bearing dust
- Short raids into sealed buildings with improvised respiratory protection (wet cloth, masks): **Possible but extremely dangerous**
- Extended scavenging without proper PPE: **Essentially suicidal** (lung cancer, years later)
- The reward would have to be extraordinary to justify the risk
- **What's there**: Belfast and other northern cities contain industrial equipment, hospital stores, military supplies, vehicles, electronics, copper, tools
- **Realistic activity**: Small, desperate, or specialized teams making fast targeted raids. They know what they're going in for, they know the routes, and they accept they're shortening their lives. Some communities may exile criminals or debtors into the zone as punishment (forced scavenging).

#### Dublin / East Coast (Cesium + Some Plutonium from Irish Sea)

- More like Chernobyl-level contamination in most areas
- **Short raids (hours to a few days) are feasible with basic precautions**: don't eat or drink anything from the zone, cover skin, wash thoroughly after, avoid obviously hot spots
- Plutonium levels lower than Northern Ireland but present near the coast
- **What's there**: Dublin was a modern European capital. Its buildings contain:
  - Medical equipment from hospitals (Mater, St James's, Beaumont)
  - Data centre campuses (Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon) full of solar panels, UPS batteries, copper cable, server racks, cooling equipment
  - University labs and libraries (Trinity, UCD, DCU)
  - Pharmaceutical manufacturing plants (Ireland was a major pharma hub)
  - Port equipment from Dublin Port
  - Weapons and ammunition from Garda and military stores
  - Tools, vehicles, building materials
- **Realistic activity**: This is probably an organised, established scavenging economy by 12 years. Teams know the contamination map (learned through experience and deaths). They specialise: one team knows the hospital route, another knows the data centre sites. There are probably territorial disputes.

#### Waterford-Limerick Band (Cesium Only, No Significant Plutonium)

- Least dangerous of the contaminated zones
- **Day trips are relatively safe** with basic precautions
- The main risk is food chain contamination (don't farm it) rather than acute radiation from visiting
- Abandoned farms, towns, and industrial sites are accessible for salvage
- **What's there**: Agricultural equipment, farm buildings, small-town retail stock, vehicles, fuel stores (degraded but some usable), building materials
- **Realistic activity**: Regular, semi-routine salvage operations from communities just south of the line. Probably normalised by 12 years — people cross into the band the way farmers might cross a mildly dangerous river. Risky but done regularly.

| Item | Where Found | Why Valuable |
|---|---|---|
| Copper wire and cable | Everywhere | Electrical systems, trade currency |
| Medical equipment and supplies | Hospitals | Surgery, diagnostics, drugs (some still viable) |
| Solar panels | Data centres, commercial buildings | Power generation |

| Laboratory equipment | Universities, pharma plants | Chemistry, water testing, medical |
| Metal (steel, aluminium, brass) | Industrial sites, construction | Manufacturing |

### The Contaminated Goods Problem

- Salvaged materials from the zones enter the clean economy
- Some communities test goods for contamination; some don't or can't
- Contaminated metal, tools, or electronics pose a long-term health risk to users
- A black market in untested goods is inevitable
- The ability to test for contamination becomes a source of power and authority
- Communities that control Geiger counters or dosimeters have an advantage

### Health Cost of Scavenging

- Scavengers develop radiation-related illness over years: increased cancer rates, respiratory disease (especially in plutonium zones), immune suppression
- This is a known cost of the trade — scavengers are the post-collapse equivalent of coal miners: essential, exploited, and slowly dying from the work
- Some scavengers may be involuntary — prisoners, debtors, or outcasts sent into the zone as forced labour
- Life expectancy for regular zone scavengers: significantly reduced, maybe 10-20 years less than non-exposed population
- The health effects create a visible social class: people who look older than they are, chronic coughs, skin conditions

### Story Implications

- Scavenging the zones is a real economy with its own specialists, routes, territorial conflicts, and moral compromises
- The Ireland character's community in the south likely depends on Dublin salvage for medical supplies, electronics, and building materials
- The Catholic network may control or tax the salvage trade — another lever of power
- Contaminated goods entering the clean zone is a constant low-level crisis
- Forced zone labour as punishment echoes the Magdalene laundries / industrial schools parallel already in the collective leader's profile
- A scavenger character would be a powerful addition to the Ireland sections: someone who knows both worlds, slowly dying from the knowledge

## Story Implications

- The fallout line is not temporary — it is a generational boundary
- Northern Ireland is gone in any practical sense; it is a permanent exclusion zone
- The Irish Sea is dead water — no fishing, no safe coastal activity; this shapes trade and movement patterns
- Dublin is a ruin or a ghost city, not a functioning capital
- Southern Ireland is genuinely one of the better places on Earth to be post-collapse: clean, agricultural, mild, isolated by water
- The Ireland character lives in a rare safe zone, which makes his art practice feel less like madness and more like a rational response to being one of the lucky ones
- The Waterford-Limerick line will shift slightly over decades as cesium decays, creating a slowly expanding habitable frontier — but within the story's timeframe it is fixed
- **Ireland was hit by EMP even though no bombs fell on Irish soil.** A high-altitude electromagnetic pulse (HEMP) over the UK — standard doctrine in a nuclear war targeting multiple UK sites — would have clear line-of-sight to the entire island of Ireland. A single 1.4 MT burst at 400km altitude covers a 1,000-1,500km radius. Ireland's mountains (highest: ~1,039m in Kerry) are too low to provide meaningful terrain shielding. This means all unshielded electronics across Ireland — north and south, contaminated and clean — were damaged or destroyed in the initial pulse. The safe zone in the south is clean from radiation but was stripped of electronics. Working technology at 12 years is only what was underground or inside metal-framed buildings at the moment of the HEMP, or what was simple enough (purely mechanical, basic electrical) to survive. Neural implants in anyone on the island were hit — implanted people in the open likely suffered seizures or brain damage from induced current through the implant's electrodes. See `notes/china--implant_technology.md` for full EMP/implant details.
- **Survivors from the north flee by boat — most to Scotland, some south to Ireland.** See `assets/ireland--northern_refugee_routes_map.png` for the route map.
  - The land route south through Northern Ireland and the border counties is a plutonium exclusion zone — days of exposure, contaminated dust, no functioning roads or settlements. Overland migration is effectively impossible.
  - The Irish Sea (east coast) is contaminated from Sellafield. The only viable sea route south is the Atlantic coast: around Malin Head and down the west coast, 350–550km depending on destination. Multi-day journey in small boats, genuinely dangerous.
  - But the crossing to **Scotland** from the Antrim coast is only ~20km (Torr Head to Mull of Kintyre). Scotland, or parts of it, is habitable. This is vastly easier and safer. **Most NI survivors who escape by sea go to Scotland.**
  - The smaller group who attempt the long route south (estimated 5,000–20,000 total arrivals over the first year or two, then it stops) launch from north coast ports (Portrush, Ballycastle) and western Donegal. Boats are fishing trawlers, pleasure craft, anything seaworthy — some overloaded, some lashed together.
  - **The refugees heading south are disproportionately Protestant/Unionist.** The Catholic population in NI was concentrated in the border counties and western areas — closer to the worst contamination paths, with higher mortality. Some surviving northern Catholics crossed to Scotland instead, fearing exclusion by the southern Catholic power structure. The result: the people who actually arrive on Ireland's west coast are mostly from the Protestant northeast.
  - **They are resisted by the Catholic network** in the south — too many mouths, wrong denomination, outsiders in a territory already squeezed between the contamination band and the French occupation.
  - Early arrivals land scattered along the west coast and are pushed out. Word spreads that **the French in Cork will accept newcomers**. Later boats sail directly to Cork Harbour.
  - By 12 years: the northern refugees are integrated into French-controlled Cork — intermarriage, shared children, a French-Irish-Northern hybrid community. See `notes/ireland--french_occupation.md` for full details.
