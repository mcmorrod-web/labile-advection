# French Occupation of Southern Ireland

## Overview

Approximately 500,000 armed French refugees have crossed the Atlantic from the Brittany/Biscay coast and taken control of a large portion of southern Ireland by force. They occupy roughly County Cork and surroundings (~4,000 km²), centred on Cork Harbour.

See `assets/ireland--french_occupation_map.png` for the territorial map.

## Why Cork

- **Cork Harbour** (Cobh) is one of the world's largest natural harbours — deep water, sheltered, accessible by sail
- The land around Cork is the most fertile in the safe zone — the Lee and Blackwater river valleys have the best agricultural soil in southern Ireland
- Cork city provides existing infrastructure: buildings, bridges, roads, water systems
- The south coast offers multiple landing points between Kinsale and Cobh
- It's far from the contamination band (the Blackwater River forms a rough natural northern boundary)

## Land Requirements

- Pre-industrial subsistence farming in Ireland's climate: ~0.5-1 hectare per person
- 500,000 people × 0.5-1 ha = 2,500-5,000 km² needed for food self-sufficiency
- County Cork is ~7,500 km² — more than enough, but they also need to actually hold and work it

## French-Occupied Territory (~4,000 km²)

### Core Zone
- **Cork City**: French HQ and administrative centre
- **Cobh / Cork Harbour**: Port and naval base; controls sea access
- **Midleton**: Eastern anchor; agricultural hinterland

### Coastal Strip (south coast)
- **Kinsale**: Fishing port and secondary harbour
- **Bandon**: Market town, river crossing
- **Clonakilty**: Agricultural centre
- **Skibbereen**: Western extent of solid control

### Inland (northern reach)
- **Mallow**: Controls the Blackwater Valley; northern frontier
- **Fermoy**: Eastern Blackwater; frontier garrison
- The Blackwater River functions as a natural defensive boundary

### Disputed / Frontier Zone
- The boundary between French and Irish territory runs roughly along the Blackwater River and the uplands between Cork and Kerry
- This is a contested zone — patrols, skirmishes, raids, and uneasy local arrangements
- Some Irish communities within French territory may remain but under French authority

## Irish-Controlled Territory

With the French holding Cork, the remaining Irish safe zone is significantly reduced:

- **Kerry**: Tralee, Killarney — likely the new centre of Irish Catholic network power
- **Clare** (south of Shannon): Ennis, the Burren
- **South Tipperary**: Reduced by contamination band from the north
- **Western Cork peninsulas**: Beara, Sheep's Head, Mizen — may be disputed or Irish-held depending on how far French control extends

The French occupation roughly doubles the population competing for the safe zone's resources. This creates:
- Food pressure (agricultural land divided, not shared)
- Political crisis (two armed populations in a small area)
- Cultural collision (language, religion, governance systems)
- The northern refugees add another layer: English-speaking Irish people integrated into the French zone, rejected by the Catholic network

## How They Got There

- Sailed from French Atlantic ports (Brest, Lorient, Saint-Nazaire, La Rochelle, Bordeaux) as France became uninhabitable
- The crossing from Brittany to Cork is ~500-600 km — manageable by sail in 3-7 days depending on conditions
- They came armed because France was a nuclear-armed state with extensive military infrastructure; retreating military units and armed civilians would have access to weapons
- The arrival was not a single event but a series of waves over months or years
- Early arrivals may have negotiated or coexisted; later waves came with force as desperation grew
- By 12 years post-collapse, the occupation is established and entrenched

## Relationship with the Irish Catholic Network

- The French occupation creates a major power struggle in the only habitable zone in Western Europe
- The Catholic network was the pre-existing authority structure; the French are an armed rival
- Possible dynamics:
  - Open conflict (territorial war between French and Irish factions)
  - Uneasy partition (the map as it stands — each side holds territory, skirmishes at the border)
  - Negotiated coexistence with tension (trade, intermarriage, but political separation)
  - French adoption of / integration into the Catholic network structure (unlikely if they arrived armed and took territory by force)
- Religion: France was historically Catholic but post-collapse French refugees may be secular, mixed, or have developed their own survival ideology
- Language: French-speaking enclave in English/Irish-speaking territory

## Northern Irish Refugees in French Cork

### Who They Are

In the first year or two after the collapse, 5,000–20,000 survivors from Northern Ireland fled south by boat down the Atlantic coast (the land route through the exclusion zone is impossible; the Irish Sea is contaminated). Most NI survivors crossed to Scotland instead (~20km from the Antrim coast), which is habitable. The smaller group who came south were disproportionately Protestant/Unionist — the Catholic population in NI was concentrated in the border counties and western areas closer to the worst contamination, with higher mortality, and some surviving northern Catholics went to Scotland instead, fearing exclusion by the southern Catholic power structure.

### Why Cork

The Catholic network in Kerry and Clare resisted the northern arrivals — too many mouths in a territory already squeezed between the contamination band and the French occupation, and the wrong denomination. Early arrivals landed scattered along the west coast (Galway, Clare, Kerry) and were pushed out: denied land, denied food, told to move on. Word spread — through returning boats, through the coastal grapevine — that the French in Cork would accept newcomers. The French had no stake in the Protestant/Catholic divide. They needed people: workers, fishermen, anyone with maritime skills or strong backs. Later boats bypassed the west coast entirely and sailed directly to Cork Harbour.

### How They Were Absorbed

The French occupation was already a foreign force in Irish territory — absorbing a few thousand northern Irish refugees cost them nothing politically (they were already hated by the Catholic network) and gained them useful labour. The northerners brought:
- **Maritime skills**: NI had a significant fishing fleet; these people knew boats, tides, nets, repair
- **Mechanical and engineering knowledge**: Belfast had been an industrial city; some refugees carried trades
- **English language**: Useful for dealing with the Irish-controlled zone, which the French-speaking population couldn't easily do
- **Motivation**: Rejected everywhere else, they had every reason to make Cork work

### Status at 12 Years

By the story's timeframe (~12 years post-collapse), the northern refugees are surprisingly integrated into French Cork:
- **Intermarriage** between French and northern Irish is common — 12 years is long enough for relationships, children, blurred lines
- **A hybrid community** is emerging: French-Irish-Northern, with its own pidgin language mixing French and English, its own customs drawn from both cultures
- The original northern Protestant identity is fading as a separate category — their children are growing up in Cork, speaking French alongside English, with French-Northern parents
- They are not a distinct political faction but a **woven-in minority**: present in the fishing crews, the harbour work, the frontier garrisons, the farms
- Some individual northerners have risen to positions of usefulness (maritime expertise, translation/liaison with Irish-controlled territory) but they don't hold political power as a bloc

### What This Means

- The French zone is more linguistically and culturally complex than a simple French enclave — it contains a significant English-speaking northern Irish component
- The Catholic network's rejection of the northerners deepens the rivalry: the French absorbed people the Catholics turned away, which is both a practical gain and a moral indictment
- The northerners' integration into French Cork makes the occupation harder to frame as purely foreign — it now contains Irish people too, just the "wrong" kind
- For the Catholic network, the northern Protestants in French Cork are a provocation: a reminder that the network's authority is sectarian, not universal
- Any future negotiation between French and Irish zones is complicated by the northerners' presence — they can't go "home" to NI (exclusion zone) and the Catholic south already rejected them

## Implications for the Ireland Character

- The Ireland character lives in the Irish-controlled zone (Kerry or Clare)
- The French occupation is the dominant political fact of his life — the neighbouring power, the territorial threat, the cultural Other
- His connection to the Catholic network (which communicates with the Wuyishan collective) gives him a role in a power structure that is competing with a French occupation
- The art practice (glass, paintings, music) may be a form of cultural resistance — insisting on a non-French, non-survival-only identity
- Salvage expeditions to Dublin now have an additional complication: do the French control any of the eastern routes?
