## The Four Hills Walk

A named local circuit at the southeast edge of Dublin, where the city runs out against Dublin Bay and the Wicklow foothills begin. The walk links four low granite hills in a single loop — **Flagstaff Hill, Dalkey Hill, Killiney Hill, and Gorse Hill** — in the ground above Killiney and Dalkey villages. Pre-collapse, this was a standard south Dublin walking circuit: a few hours in all weathers, a lifetime's worth of afternoons for people who lived near enough to treat it as the local ground.

### The four

- **Flagstaff Hill.** The summit with the flagpole, holding the widest view of the circuit — Dublin Bay north to Howth, Killiney Bay south to Bray Head, the Wicklow Mountains west, the Irish Sea east.
- **Dalkey Hill.** Granite quarry on the seaward face, quarrymen's steps, the summit telegraph, Dalkey Island below. The northern anchor of the walk.
- **Killiney Hill.** The wooded summit with the obelisk (the Killiney Obelisk, 1742), the paths through pines and gorse, views down over Killiney Bay.
- **Gorse Hill.** The fourth summit in the circuit — whin in flower in May, thin soil on granite, the sea immediately below, the most exposed leg of the walk.

Summits around 130–160 m; the whole walk a few kilometres. None of it is a serious climb. That is the point. This was **everyday ground** — the place a person walks in all weathers across a life, the place that is the first thought when they think of the word *hill*.

### Why this ground mattered to the character

Specific features a child or a young adult walking the Four Hills would have known:
- The Killiney Obelisk; the Flagstaff and its pole; the concrete benches worn smooth; the granite kerbs on the summit paths
- The granite of Dalkey quarry, sheer on the bay side, with climbing routes bolted pre-collapse; the quarrymen's steps
- Gorse in flower, yellow across Gorse Hill in May; whitethorn; blackthorn slá
- The Vico Road swimming spot below, the Forty Foot around the corner at Sandycove
- The DART line running below along the coast; the trains visible from the hill; the sound of them in calm weather
- Bray Head and the Sugar Loaf to the south, the Wicklow Mountains behind, the sea to the east, Howth across the bay to the north
- The pines at the top of Killiney, the brambles on the quarry paths, the gorse scratching bare legs
- The link paths between the four — the short cols between the summits that turn four separate hills into a single loop

A character who grew up walking this circuit carries it as **the body's default landscape** — what the phrase "going for a walk" still means in their head twelve years into the collapse, even as they live in Kerry or Clare.

## The Dalkey–Blackrock Routine

The character is from **Dalkey**. Before the collapse, the hills were only one part of his ordinary geography. His recurring journey in the other direction followed the coast north to **Blackrock**:

- By the final pre-collapse years, the DART coastal corridor had been rebuilt as an urban **maglev** line. The old name survived the technology: everyone still called it the DART.
- He usually travelled later than the morning peak, deliberately letting the commuter crowd clear before boarding at Dalkey.
- The purpose was habitual rather than exceptional: yoga in Blackrock. On rest days, when he did not train, he went to a sauna there instead.
- The routine gives him a bodily map of the old city beyond the Four Hills — platform timing, the coast sliding past the carriage, the quieter late train, heat and recovery at the destination.

Blackrock's coastline had also changed. Reclamation and flood engineering pushed a series of public walls and breakwaters well out into Dublin Bay. Their tops became long pedestrian routes over the water: exposed, linear walks with the city behind and an open eastern horizon ahead. The character went there for sunrise. Seen from the ends of those walls, first light came across the bay before it reached the streets.

This is not grand tourism in his memory. Like the Four Hills, it is repeated local practice: yoga, sauna, a late DART, a walk along engineered stone and concrete to watch the day arrive. The loss is sharpened because these were places he used without thinking they would ever need to be remembered.

## Post-Collapse Status of the Ground

The four hills sit inside the east-coast contamination strip (`notes/ireland--fallout_research.md`). They are in the same zone as Dublin proper — the Irish Sea at their feet, Sellafield due east across the water, every easterly fallout event depositing onto the granite and the gorse. The hills themselves are structurally intact: they are granite, they will be granite in ten thousand years. But:

- Nobody lives there. The coastal strip from Sandycove through Dalkey and Killiney to Bray has been depopulated since the first year.
- The gorse has spread. With no grazing and no path maintenance, twelve years of vegetation has closed over the circuit. Gorse Hill is more gorse than path. The link cols between the summits are barely discernible.
- The Killiney Obelisk is still standing. The Flagstaff pole probably still has its pole; the flag will have shredded and gone.
- The DART line is dead infrastructure. The tunnel through Dalkey Hill is dark. The stations are salvaged.
- The granite of Dalkey quarry is still there; the climbing bolts have rusted.
- Salvage presence is light compared with the city core — these are residential hills, not commercial, and the grand houses along the coast road have been worked through in earlier years. What draws occasional attention now is the deeper quiet salvage: a specific house with specific objects, or a view from a specific summit that somebody wants to see again.
- No Catholic-zone authority reaches here. Crews that come are from Dublin's own thinning scavenger population, or from independent operators running the east coast.

For the character, the ground still exists, physically. It is reachable. The question is only whether it is recognisable — and whether the body that would recognise it is allowed to come close.

## The Footage

The character has old footage of the Four Hills. Setting it out:

- **Phone video or small-camera footage**, taken pre-collapse. A decade and a half or two decades old by year 12.
- **Held on salvaged storage** — probably on a memory card or small hard drive that survived the HEMP because it was physically small and metal-cased, or stored inside a metal box, or shielded by the building it was in. Flash storage is generally durable over this timeframe if the card itself wasn't fried by induced current.
- **Played back** on salvaged hardware — a small screen and a battery, assembled by someone who knows electronics. The Catholic safe zone doesn't have a general computing culture at year 12 (`notes/ireland--fallout_research.md` power-and-energy section — post-electric for most of the population) but surviving tablets, laptops, and small displays exist, and communities with micro-hydro have the power budget to run them occasionally. A character watching footage is watching a salvaged screen run on battery salvaged from Poolbeg or from a solar setup somewhere.
- **Watched alone** or with a small trusted audience. This is not public viewing. Pre-collapse footage is a private artefact — both because of the emotional charge and because public display risks attention from the parish (the same logic that keeps his Sligo trips quiet).
- **Domestic, not composed.** Phone clips meant to be looked at once and forgotten. Shaky, ambient audio, casual framing. These are the kind of images everyone had before the war and almost nobody still has.

Watching the footage is a practice adjacent to his glass-and-painting work (`characters/ireland--character.md`). Both are acts of inserting himself, retroactively, into a world that no longer exists. The glass lets him be inside a Vermeer or a Hammershøi reflection. The footage lets him be back on Dalkey Hill. The mechanisms differ; the impulse is continuous.

### Craft rule: withhold the image

**The footage is never rendered on the page.** This is a locked structural discipline. The novel does not describe what the screen shows. It describes the room, the small weight of the screen on his lap, the sound of the playback through degraded speakers, the battery temperature, the light of the display on his face, the hand on the bench beside the device — never the image itself. The Sebald grammar: the picture is there and the text does not explain it. The reader does the looking.

This applies equally to:

- The pre-collapse footage of the Four Hills Walk (before any flyby)
- The year-12 drone footage (if a flyby happens)
- Any moment where the two sit side by side on playback

If the reader needs to know what's on a clip, they infer it from what the character does — pauses it, rewinds it, stops it, turns it off, puts his face in his hands, walks out of the room. The content of the image is always offstage.

The discipline protects the scene from every sentimentality trap available. The moment the prose starts describing the image on the screen, the scene becomes a before-and-after montage and competes with a century of film elegies it cannot win. Held offstage, the image retains the one thing a drone-and-footage set-piece has to earn: unbearable specificity that the reader has to build for themselves.

## The Drone Flyby

The possibility, at year 12, of putting a small drone up over the four hills and recording new footage that can be set against the old.

### Why this is a significant story beat

Working drones are rare on the island. Ireland was fully HEMP-saturated (`planning/world_timeline.md`; `notes/china--implant_technology.md`) and every unshielded electronic device on the island was destroyed in the initial pulse. A working drone at year 12 requires:

1. **A surviving airframe and electronics** — a drone that was either deeply shielded (inside a safe, underground, in a metal-framed building away from windows) at the moment of the HEMP, or an airframe that was brought into Ireland from elsewhere later (extremely unlikely — intercontinental physical trade is dead, `notes/trade_and_communication_research.md`).
2. **Working batteries** — lithium-polymer drone batteries have a shelf life of a few years even in storage. Pre-collapse stored batteries are almost certainly dead at year 12. Fresh batteries require salvaged industrial UPS cells or salvaged lithium packs — most plausibly sourced from **Poolbeg Island** (`notes/ireland--dublin.md`) through the salvage trade.
3. **Someone who can repair and fly it.** A surviving drone without a working operator is a paperweight. Pre-collapse drone operators (aerial photographers, surveyors, hobbyists, delivery pilots) still exist but are scattered; one of them connected to the character is a specific fact the story would need.
4. **A flight plan that works without GPS.** GPS satellites are mostly gone or degraded (`notes/trade_and_communication_research.md`). Any drone flight at year 12 is visual line-of-sight, manual control, inertial dead-reckoning — much harder than pre-collapse recreational flying.
5. **An acceptable risk of loss.** The drone will plausibly not come back. Wind over Killiney Head from the south or southeast, any electronics glitch, a lost signal at the edge of control range, a contamination exposure that damages components — any of these end the flight. The drone is, in a real sense, single-use hardware.

All of this means a drone flyby over the south Dublin hills is not a casual undertaking. It is a planned expedition, using rare salvaged parts, with a recognised risk of losing the only working piece of aerial-imaging equipment the character is ever likely to hold.

### The human picture

The character wants to see the hills as they are now, from above, in moving image. Not because it will tell him anything he doesn't already know — the ground is contaminated, depopulated, overgrown; he knows all this. Because:

- He wants an image he can set against the old footage. A before-and-after, not for anyone else, for himself.
- He wants to see the gorse. Twelve years of unchecked growth on those slopes. What does it look like. What has it done to the paths.
- He wants to see the obelisk and the flagpole. He wants to know whether they are still there.
- He wants to fly over the house he grew up in or the house a loved one lived in. He wants to see its roof. Whether the slates are still on. Whether the garden has gone to the wild.
- He wants the drone to do what his own body cannot do without cost — go into the contamination zone, look, come back. The drone is a proxy for the walk he is never going to take again.

### The flight itself

Working sketch of a plausible operation:

- **Launch point**. The drone needs to take off from ground the character can reach, within its round-trip range. A launch from the Wicklow uplands — the southern margin of the east-coast strip, reachable overland from the Catholic zone via the interior — puts the south Dublin hills within a few kilometres. A launch from a boat off Killiney Bay is the other plausible option. The boat-launch version needs a French Cork crew or independent maritime operator willing to hold position in contaminated waters.
- **Range**. Twelve years post-collapse, a drone on salvaged batteries is running well below its original factory specifications. Realistic flight time: 10–20 minutes. Range: a few kilometres at best. The character doesn't get a grand tour; he gets a single controlled pass over the cluster and a flight back.
- **Imagery**. On-board video recording to whatever small-format storage the drone carries. The flight plan traces the Four Hills Walk from the air — Flagstaff, Dalkey, Killiney, Gorse, in the order the character walked them. Live downlink is possible if the operator is in range but uncertain — radio interference, degraded components, the sheer lack of redundancy. The plan may be simply to recover the drone and pull the storage.
- **Loss scenario**. If the drone doesn't come back, the character has the flight attempt and nothing else. No footage, a lost irreplaceable machine, and the uncomfortable fact that somewhere out there in the gorse on one of the four summits or in the cold sea below is a small dead object with his ambition inside it.
- **Success scenario**. The drone returns. The storage is pulled. The footage is watched. **Per the craft rule above, what the footage shows is never rendered on the page.** The scene lives in the room around the screen: his face, the operator's face, the sound through the speakers, the pause, the rewind, the point at which he turns it off. The reader builds the image.

### Whose drone and whose battery

Two plausible provenances, which have different story weights:

- **An Irish-side drone, Irish-salvaged battery.** Someone the character knows — likely through the Catholic network or through the salvage economy — has kept a drone alive since the collapse and acquired a fresh Poolbeg battery for this flight. This roots the scene in the Irish economy entirely: the character's memory, a local operator, a Poolbeg-sourced cell, a Wicklow launch point. Clean structural coherence with the existing world.
- **A collective-network drone.** The Wuyishan collective's drones (`notes/china--collective_network.md`) are the most numerous working drones on Earth, but they are in southern China, and the story has established that intercontinental physical trade is dead. **A working drone from the collectives does not get to Ireland by any plausible means.** So the drone is not from Wuyishan. But the *knowledge* of how to get a drone flying again — specific technical knowledge about shielded-hardware revival, about battery-cell substitution, about blind flight — could plausibly travel over the Catholic-Wuyishan radio link (`notes/trade_and_communication_research.md`). In this version, the drone is Irish hardware but the repair protocol came over the wire. The character's flight over his childhood hills is, at a level he may or may not understand, an act made possible by a Chinese collective's surviving technical culture. This is a structurally interesting version because it puts the AI network quietly inside an apparently private Irish grief.

Default for now: **Irish-side hardware, Irish battery, Irish operator**, with the option of the repair-knowledge link to the collectives as a deeper layer if the novel wants it.

## Story Uses

### As a chapter

A single quiet chapter carries the flight:

1. **The preparation.** The character acquires the battery; meets the operator; walks into the Wicklow foothills with a small party; selects a launch point on a clear morning.
2. **The pre-flight.** Battery warmed, airframe checked, GPS confirmed absent, compass confirmed working, wind assessed, the sea silvered below. Somebody says something dry. Somebody else doesn't speak at all.
3. **The launch.** The drone lifts. The character watches it climb and bank out over the contamination zone, taking the line of the old walk — Flagstaff first, then Dalkey, then Killiney, then Gorse.
4. **The wait.** The operator hunched over a small display in a coat. The reader is not shown the downlink. Everybody else is watching the sky. The sound of the drone receding. Minutes.
5. **The return (or the non-return).** Either the drone is heard again and descends, or it doesn't come back. The chapter holds the same weight either way; the one with the return gets a second half on the ground where the footage is pulled and played back.
6. **The playback.** Either that evening or days later, when hardware and power permit. The old footage and the new. The content of both is held offstage (see the craft rule under "The Footage"). The chapter ends on the character — his face, a hand on the bench, the device closed, somebody else across the room — not on the final frame of anything.

This is a McCarthy-register chapter. The tone is quiet. The emotional load sits in the specifics of the room and the weather and the body — the battery temperature, the operator's hands, the crossing and recrossing of the line where the drone is no longer audible, the moment before the drone is audible again. The image itself is never on the page. That is the discipline.

### Seeded chapter opening: the drone's view as equipment POV

The novel's existing structural device — a short equipment-POV passage at the top of each chapter (`planning/tone_and_style.md`, "Chapter Opening Device: Equipment Perspective") — is the right home for the drone's own view of the Four Hills.

The decision: **an equipment-POV opening carrying the drone's view of the Four Hills Walk is seeded into the novel well before the flight chapter.** It reads as one more ambient surveillance passage — granite, gorse, an obelisk from above, the sea, wind direction — in the register the reader has been learning to read for chapters already. Unlabelled. Unattributed. Just another of the book's equipment observations.

The reader does not know, on first read:

- That this is aerial footage
- That it is a drone's view
- That it is the Four Hills Walk
- That it is the character's childhood ground

The recognition comes later. When the flight chapter arrives — the preparation, the launch, the wait, the return — the reader understands, at the moment the storage is pulled and the footage is watched (offstage), that they have already read it. The equipment opening seeded earlier was the footage. The drone's view has already been in the book. The character's most private act of looking has been, all along, one of the novel's surveillance passages.

This makes the scene specifically this novel's. No other book can execute it this way, because no other book has the equipment-POV chain to slot the drone's view into.

Notes on execution:

- The seeded opening must read seamlessly as an equipment passage. No tells. The reader should not think "drone" on first read; they should think "one more of those openings". Granite, whin, the Killiney Obelisk named only as *a concrete obelisk on a wooded summit*, the sea, the salt in the wind, the corrosion on a metal pole, the absence of people.
- Placement: at the top of a chapter that has nothing to do with Ireland on the surface — ideally a China or Moon chapter. The reader reads the opening without any framing that would connect it to the Four Hills. The disconnect is the point; the connection is the payoff.
- The recognition must be left to the reader. The flight chapter should not spell out "the opening you read three chapters ago was this footage". It should trust the reader to feel the click when the footage is pulled. Some readers will feel it in the moment; others on a reread. Both are correct.
- The recursion connects to the deeper layer: if the drone was repaired using knowledge that came over the Catholic-Wuyishan radio link, then the drone is, in a real sense, a field instrument of the AI network. The equipment-POV opening is not a metaphor. The drone's view *is* the AI's view *is* the book's surveillance voice. This is the fullest version of the recursion and the one the novel is structurally already set up for.

This combines with **withhold the image** cleanly. The equipment opening *renders* the aerial view in the equipment register — spare, observational, data-like — without ever identifying it as photographable content or as footage being watched on a screen elsewhere. The *screen* is always offstage. The equipment passage is not the screen; it is the drone's own attention, which the novel's frame already admits as a voice. These two disciplines do not conflict; they depend on each other.

### As character backstory even without the flight

The Four Hills work as character geography whether or not the drone flight happens. Having a character whose mental default landscape is Flagstaff / Dalkey / Killiney / Gorse gives the Ireland strand:

- A **displacement**. He is in Kerry or Clare but he is from south Dublin. That is a specific pre-collapse life — suburban, coastal, tech-adjacent or professional-adjacent, probably educated through Trinity or UCD, probably working in or near the pre-collapse Dublin economy. It makes his current location a survival outcome, not a birth fact.
- A **grief that has a location**. The character doesn't just miss a pre-collapse world; he misses a specific ground, which still physically exists but cannot be walked. This is a sharper grief than nostalgia.
- A **connection to the Dublin contamination zone**. The salvage economy (`notes/ireland--dublin.md`) is not a distant background fact for him. His hills are in it. His childhood house is in it.
- A **rhyme with the art practice** (`characters/ireland--character.md`). The glass-and-painting ritual inserts him into a pre-collapse artwork; the drone flyby, if it happens, lets him insert an instrument of his own into a pre-collapse place. Both are structurally the same move — placing a small mediating device between himself and a vanished world so that he can re-enter it.

### As foreshadowing or pay-off

The footage watching could appear early — a short scene, the character alone with a small screen and a battery, watching gorse and flagpole, not telling the reader yet what he is watching. The drone flyby, if it comes, pays off that earlier scene. The chapter could also be structured so that the flyby is attempted and fails, and the footage-watching scenes continue unchanged afterwards — the failure is its own ending, and the character goes on with only the old image.

## Who This Character Is

Open question, flagged carefully:

- **Default reading: the existing Ireland character** (`characters/ireland--character.md`). The artist. South Dublin origin, displaced to Kerry or Clare after the collapse. The Four Hills footage is private in the same way his glass-and-painting practice is private. The drone flyby, if attempted, is the outward-facing twin of the inward-facing art ritual. The two practices rhyme. This reading fills in a gap in the character file (his origin, not previously specified) and deepens the practice's meaning (he is not a general griever; he is grieving one place).
- **Alternative: a second Ireland character, perhaps a second POV.** The novel's main strands are Ireland artist, China, Moon. A second Irish voice — specifically, someone from east-coast Dublin, displaced into the safe zone — would expand the Ireland section and give the novel a non-artist Irish viewpoint. If this is a separate character, the drone-flyby scene could be one of the ways the two Irish characters meet: the artist knows a drone operator with south-Dublin memories, or the drone operator is the artist's neighbour in Kerry, or they appear in each other's chapters around the flight.
- **Low-probability third: a pre-collapse scene.** The footage itself could anchor a prequel passage — the filming of it — which would be a way of showing south Dublin in the last years before the war without the main novel's characters having to narrate it.

I would default to the first reading and develop the second only if the novel wants more Ireland voice than the artist alone can carry.

## Open Questions

- **Attribution.** Existing Ireland character, or a second Irish character (possibly a second POV). Default is the existing character; the alternative is genuinely open.
- **Where in Dalkey the character lived.** The specific street or townland gives specific salvage temptation (his own house, his parent's house, a sibling's flat).
- **The order of the walk.** Which of the four hills he started on matters — the body's default direction (Flagstaff → Dalkey → Killiney → Gorse, or reversed, or looping) is a piece of specific sense-memory.
- **Who the other people in the footage are.** A parent, a sibling, a partner, a friend. The reader will not see them, but the character will hear them offstage, and his reactions will convey who they were. A sibling lost in the collapse rhymes with Tian Hongpao's sibling thread in the China strand.
- **The drone's operator.** A specific person with a specific history — pre-collapse drone pilot, aerial photographer, surveyor, delivery operator — who keeps this machine alive. This person is a character in their own right if the flight becomes a chapter.
- **The battery provenance.** Irish-salvaged Poolbeg cell; a salvaged UPS from a Dublin hospital; something from a Three Rock corporate clinic's backup system. The provenance is a small but specific fact.
- **Whether the flight happens.** The novel can hold the possibility without ever executing it. The character can talk about it, plan it, almost attempt it, and never fly. This is a valid structural answer — the drone flight as unfulfilled intention. If so, the seeded equipment-POV opening is not a drone at all but a piece of the novel's general surveillance voice that the reader retrospectively reinterprets differently.
- **Whether the flight succeeds.** If it is attempted: recovery of the drone and footage, or loss. Both carry weight. Loss is the more novel-shaped outcome; success is the more emotionally useful.
- **Placement of the seeded opening.** Which chapter carries the opening that is secretly the drone's view. Default: a China or Moon chapter well before the Ireland flight sequence. Specific placement depends on chapter order.
- **The repair-knowledge link.** Does the technical knowledge that enables the flight come over the Catholic-zone / Wuyishan radio link? If yes, this fuses the drone-POV opening with the AI-network-POV opening: the drone's view *is* the AI's view, not by analogy but by fact. This is the fullest version of the recursion. (Open question — but the novel's existing structure already supports it.)
- **Who else watches the footage with him.** Nobody, a confessor, a confidant, a child in Kerry who has never seen a city. The third is striking — a child in the room with a salvaged screen, seeing something the character does not describe aloud.
