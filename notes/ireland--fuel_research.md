# Ireland Fuel Sources — Post-Collapse (~12 Years)

Ireland imports over 81% of its energy. With global trade collapsed, the question is what fuel exists on the island without imports.

---

## 1. Peat (Turf)

Ireland's most important indigenous fuel for centuries. When wood ran out in the 1600s, peat became the default. During WWII, when imports were rationed, the government mobilised industrial peat harvesting to replace coal.

### What exists

- **Bord na Mona landbank**: ~200,000 acres (80,000 hectares) across ~130 bogs
- **Main concentrations**: Offaly, Laois, Kildare, Westmeath, Longford, Roscommon, East Galway — the midland raised bogs
- **Also significant**: Kerry, Mayo, Donegal, Clare, Galway — blanket bogs on uplands and western coasts
- **Peat-burning power stations** (pre-collapse): Edenderry (Offaly), Lough Ree (Longford), West Offaly (Shannon)

### Problem for the story

The biggest industrial peat reserves are in the **midlands** — which is in or near the contamination zone (east of the safe zone, potentially within the Dublin/east coast fallout shadow). The midlands bogs are borderline: not in the worst exclusion zone but not clearly safe either.

### What the safe zone has

- **Kerry blanket bogs**: Significant. Hand-cut turf has been harvested here for centuries. Not industrial scale but enough for heating and cooking across rural communities.
- **Clare bogs**: Moderate. Some blanket bog in the Burren margins and uplands.
- **West Cork bogs**: Some, but now French-held territory.

### Verdict

Peat is the primary heating and cooking fuel for the safe zone. Hand-cutting with a slean (traditional tool) requires no industrial infrastructure. Every rural Irish person historically knew how to do this. At 12 years post-collapse, this skill would have been relearned by necessity. **Not enough for industrial use or power generation, but sufficient for domestic heating and cooking.**

## 2. Wood and Timber

### What exists

- **Total forest cover**: ~770,000 hectares (11% of land area)
- **Mostly Sitka spruce plantation** (52%+ of all forest) — fast-growing, wet-climate adapted
- **Native woodland**: ~140,000 hectares (2% of land area)
- **Annual timber output** (pre-collapse): 1.4 million m³ Sitka spruce logs
- **Biomass potential**: Short rotation coppice (willow) yields 10-12 tonnes dry matter per hectare per year

### What the safe zone has

Kerry, Clare, and Tipperary all have significant plantation forestry. Kerry's mountains and valleys have both plantation spruce and native oak/birch woodland. Cork (French-held) has extensive forest.

### Uses

- **Heating and cooking**: Direct burning. The most basic fuel.
- **Charcoal**: Can be made from wood in simple kilns. Burns hotter and cleaner than raw wood. Essential for metalworking and smithing.
- **Steam power**: Wood can fire steam boilers (see rail section below).
- **Construction**: Timber is also the primary building material — fuel use competes with structural use.

### Verdict

Wood is abundant in the safe zone and does not require any industrial infrastructure to harvest. **The main constraint is not supply but sustainability** — aggressive logging without replanting will strip the landscape within a generation. Managed coppicing (cutting and regrowth) is the sustainable approach and was historically practiced.

## 3. Coal

### Ireland's coalfields

| Coalfield | Location | Status at closure | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Arigna** | Co. Roscommon / Leitrim | Closed 1990, reserves depleted | Powered ESB station (55,000 tonnes/year at peak) |
| **Castlecomer** | Co. Kilkenny | Closed | Leinster coalfield, anthracite |
| **Ballingarry / Slieve Ardagh** | Co. Tipperary | Closed 1989, mines flooded | Anthracite, reached 50,000 tonnes/year in 1840s |
| **Kanturk** | North Cork | Small, historically minor | Limited extraction |

### Problem for the story

- **Arigna** (Roscommon): Borderline zone. Accessible from the west (Galway/Clare side) but reserves were depleted by 1990.
- **Castlecomer** (Kilkenny): In or near the contamination band. Dangerous to access.
- **Ballingarry / Slieve Ardagh** (Tipperary): Southern Tipperary — possibly in the safe zone margin but mines are flooded. Reopening would require massive effort.
- **Kanturk** (North Cork): French-occupied territory.

### Verdict

**Coal is effectively unavailable.** The reserves were already depleted or marginal before collapse, the mines are flooded, and most are in contaminated or French-held zones. Not a viable fuel source for the Irish safe zone.

## 4. Hydroelectric Power

### What exists

- **Ardnacrusha** (Shannon): 86 MW, Ireland's largest hydro station. 4 turbines, 28.5m head. Near Limerick city.
- **Other ESB hydro stations**: Erne scheme (Donegal/Fermanagh — Northern Ireland, exclusion zone), Liffey scheme (Dublin area — contaminated), Lee scheme (Cork — French-held), Clady (Donegal — possibly accessible), various small stations
- **Micro-hydro potential**: Kerry's rivers (Laune, Flesk, Caragh, Maine) have significant gradient. Clare's rivers less so.

### Critical question: Ardnacrusha

Ardnacrusha sits on the Shannon near Limerick — right at the edge of the contamination band. Its status depends on:
- Whether Limerick city is habitable (borderline in our scenario)
- Whether the turbines still function at 12 years without manufacturer maintenance
- Whether the dam and headrace canal are intact

If Ardnacrusha works, it is **by far the most important single piece of infrastructure in surviving Ireland** — 86 MW can power a small civilisation. Even at degraded capacity (say 30-50% with no spare parts), it provides more electricity than everything else combined.

### Verdict

**Ardnacrusha is the prize.** Whoever controls it controls the power supply. It needs no fuel — just flowing water and functioning turbines. Its location on the contamination band edge makes it contested and dangerous to maintain. Micro-hydro from Kerry's rivers provides small-scale local power (enough for a workshop, a mill, lighting for a community).

## 5. Wind Power

### What exists

- **5,000+ MW** installed capacity across 300+ wind farms (pre-collapse)
- **Top counties**: Kerry (#1), Cork (#2), Galway (#3), Clare (246 MW)
- Kerry alone has hundreds of MW of installed wind capacity

### The degradation problem

Wind turbines are high-tech machines. At 12 years without:
- Lubricants (imported)
- Replacement gearboxes, bearings, blade repairs
- Electronic control systems and grid connection
- Crane access for nacelle-level repairs

Most large turbines will have failed. Gearbox failure is the most common — typically occurs at 7-12 years even with maintenance.

### What might survive

- **Smaller, simpler turbines** with direct-drive generators (no gearbox) may still work
- Turbines that were **new at collapse** have the best chance
- Communities near wind farms may have **cannibalised parts** from failed turbines to keep a few running
- Even one working turbine provides significant power for a local community (1-3 MW)

### Verdict

**Patchy but significant.** Kerry's wind infrastructure gives the Irish safe zone an advantage. Perhaps 5-15% of pre-collapse wind capacity might still function at 12 years through aggressive parts scavenging and community maintenance. This is still substantial — potentially hundreds of MW across the safe zone. **The constraint is spare parts, not wind.**

## 6. Oil and Gas

### What exists on the island

- **Corrib gas field** (off Mayo coast): Had only a few years of reserves left pre-collapse. By 12 years post-collapse: exhausted, platform abandoned or damaged.
- **Kinsale Head gas** (off Cork): Already exhausted before collapse.
- **Barryroe oil field** (off Cork): Never developed. 311 million barrels of recoverable oil in the seabed — but requires deepwater drilling infrastructure that does not exist post-collapse.

### Verdict

**Zero.** Ireland has no accessible oil or gas without offshore drilling platforms and supply chains. Diesel, petrol, kerosene, and natural gas are all gone. This is the single most devastating loss — it eliminates motorised transport, most machinery, and industrial heating at a stroke.

## 7. Animal Fat (Tallow)

### What exists

Ireland's primary agricultural activity is cattle farming. Cattle produce:
- **Tallow**: Rendered fat, liquid when warm, solid when cool. Burns cleanly.
- **Historical use**: Candles, lamp oil, waterproofing, soap. Used for centuries as primary lighting fuel.
- **Modern research**: Tallow can substitute for diesel in stationary engines when preheated to 90°C. It can also be converted to biodiesel esters.

### Practical uses

- **Lighting**: Tallow candles and oil lamps. The primary indoor lighting source.
- **Lubrication**: Tallow grease for machinery, wheels, bearings.
- **Engine fuel**: In small quantities, preheated tallow can run a diesel engine (generator, pump). Not practical for transport at scale.
- **Soap**: Essential for sanitation.

### Verdict

**Important but limited.** Tallow is a byproduct of the cattle economy that already exists. It provides lighting and lubrication. It cannot replace diesel at transport scale. Every household would be rendering tallow as a matter of course.

## 8. Rapeseed Oil and Vegetable Oils

### What exists

Rapeseed (canola) is already grown in Ireland and is one of the crops in the Wuyishan collective too. It can be:
- Pressed for oil (lighting, cooking, lubrication)
- Converted to crude biodiesel
- Used directly in adapted diesel engines

### The land problem

Rapeseed yields ~1,000-1,500 litres of oil per hectare. To fuel even a small fleet of vehicles or machines, you need significant dedicated acreage — acreage that competes with food production. In a food-stressed safe zone, growing fuel crops is a hard political choice.

### Verdict

**Niche.** Some rapeseed grown for cooking oil and lamp fuel. Not enough to power transport. A few litres of biodiesel for a critical generator or pump — not for driving trucks.

---

## 9. Biogas (Methane)

### What exists

Anaerobic digesters can convert cattle manure, crop waste, and food waste into methane gas. The technology is simple — a sealed tank, a gas collection system, a burner. Medieval-level engineering.

### Practical uses

- Cooking gas (replaces bottled gas / natural gas)
- Lighting (gas lamps)
- Small-scale electricity generation (biogas generator)

### Verdict

**Underrated and practical.** Any farming community with cattle can build a basic digester. Provides cooking fuel and potentially small-scale electricity. Several communities in the safe zone would likely have built these by 12 years post-collapse.

---

## 10. Alcohol (Ethanol)

### What exists

Ethanol can be distilled from grain, potatoes, sugar beet, or fruit. Ireland has all of these. The distillation technology is simple and well-known (poitín — illegal distilled spirit — has been made in Ireland for centuries).

### Practical uses

- **Lamp fuel**: Burns cleanly
- **Engine fuel**: Can run spark-ignition (petrol) engines, though not diesel
- **Medical**: Antiseptic, sterilisation
- **Solvent**: Cleaning, preservation

### Verdict

**Useful but competes with food and drink.** Some ethanol production is likely for medical, lighting, and morale purposes. Not enough to power transport. The knowledge to make it is culturally embedded in Ireland (poitín tradition).

## Summary: Fuel Landscape of the Irish Safe Zone

| Fuel | Available? | Scale | Primary Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Peat** | Yes (Kerry/Clare bogs) | Domestic | Heating, cooking |
| **Wood** | Yes (plantation + native) | Moderate | Heating, cooking, charcoal, steam |
| **Coal** | No (depleted, wrong zones) | None | — |
| **Hydro (Ardnacrusha)** | Maybe (contamination edge) | Large if functional | Electricity for region |
| **Hydro (micro)** | Yes (Kerry rivers) | Small/local | Workshop power, milling, lighting |
| **Wind** | Degrading (5-15% surviving) | Moderate | Electricity (patchy) |
| **Oil/Gas** | No | None | — |
| **Tallow** | Yes (cattle byproduct) | Small | Lighting, lubrication, soap |
| **Rapeseed oil** | Limited (competes with food) | Small | Lighting, cooking oil |
| **Biogas** | Yes (digesters buildable) | Small/local | Cooking, some electricity |
| **Ethanol** | Yes (grain/potato distillation) | Small | Lamp fuel, medical, morale |

### The big picture

The safe zone runs on **peat, wood, and whatever electricity survives from wind and hydro**. There is no oil, no gas, no coal, no diesel. Motorised transport is effectively dead unless someone has built a working biodiesel or ethanol system at scale (unlikely — the land can't spare the crops).

This means:
- **Rail**: Only viable if converted to steam (wood or peat fired). See `notes/ireland_rail_research.md`.
- **Road transport**: Horse, donkey, ox-cart. Bicycles.
- **Sea transport**: Sail. Ireland returns to a maritime culture out of necessity.
- **Agriculture**: Manual and animal-powered. No tractors.
- **Heating**: Peat and wood fires. Universal.
- **Lighting**: Tallow candles, rapeseed oil lamps, occasional electric light from wind/hydro.

### Strategic implications

- **Ardnacrusha** is the single most strategically important site in Ireland. Control of the Shannon dam = control of electricity.
- **Kerry's wind farms** give the Irish safe zone a significant power advantage if turbines can be maintained.
- **French-held Cork** has wind farms and the Lee hydroelectric scheme — they are not powerless.
- **The absence of liquid fuel** is the defining constraint. It shapes everything: transport, agriculture, warfare, communication.
